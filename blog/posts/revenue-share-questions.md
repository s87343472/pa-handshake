**Short version.** Most revenue-share deals between indie builders don't fail because someone lied. They fail because nobody asked the dull questions early: what counts as revenue, who can see the numbers, who else has a claim, and how either side can walk away. Ask these seven before the first call. Your AI can do the reading and send the questions for you, and it will never agree to anything on your behalf.

---

Someone DMs you on X. They built an app, it has users, and they want you to run growth for a share of revenue. It sounds fair: no upfront cost for them, upside for you.

Six months later the share is 20% of a number you can't see, after deductions you didn't know about, from a product the founder just paused. Nobody acted in bad faith. The deal was just never written down in enough detail to fail early.

Here are the questions that would have caught it.

## 1. How exactly is revenue counted?

"20% of revenue" means very different things depending on what comes off first. App store fees (15 to 30%), refunds, payment fees, ad spend, VAT. Is it one product or everything the company sells? Does a new product next year count?

**A good answer** names the line items: "Net of store fees and refunds, on the iOS and Android app only."

**A warning sign** is "we'll figure that out later." Later is when you have the least leverage.

## 2. Can I see the numbers myself?

If your income depends on a number, you should be able to look at it, not be told it. Most stores and payment tools offer read-only access: App Store Connect, Google Play Console, Stripe.

Evidence has a pecking order. Strongest is something the platform itself shows you. Then something you look at yourself through read-only access. Then a long public history. A screenshot is the weakest: it's the easiest thing to edit.

**A good answer** is an offer of read-only access, or a short screen share where you choose what to click.

**A warning sign** is "I'll send you a monthly report."

## 3. Who else has a claim on this revenue?

Co-founders, early investors, an agency that took equity, a previous growth partner with a share that never ended. Each of these comes out of the same pot.

**A good answer** is a plain list, even if it's "nobody."

**A warning sign** is a pause, then "it's complicated."

## 4. What happened with the last person in this role?

If they've had a growth partner, marketer or co-founder before, ask what happened. People leave for good reasons. But the story tells you how this founder handles disagreements, and whether that person is still owed something.

**A good answer** is specific and doesn't blame everything on the other person.

**A warning sign** is a string of partners who "didn't work out," with nothing in writing.

## 5. Who decides what?

You'll be judged on growth, so you need some say over the things that drive it: pricing, onboarding, the ad budget, maybe the roadmap. Agree who decides what, and how much time each of you puts in.

**A good answer** separates "you decide," "I decide" and "we decide together."

**A warning sign** is full responsibility for results with no say over the product.

## 6. When and how do I get paid, and for how long?

Monthly or quarterly? From which account, in which currency? Is there a minimum? And the one people forget: if the partnership ends, do you keep earning on the users you brought in, and for how long?

**A good answer** puts dates and a tail period in writing ("12 months after the end").

**A warning sign** is that the payout depends on a decision made later by one side.

## 7. How do either of us stop?

Every partnership needs an exit that doesn't require a fight. Notice period, what happens to the accounts and content you built, what you're owed.

**A good answer** includes a trial: one small, paid or clearly scoped task before the long-term deal.

**A warning sign** is a long commitment from day one.

## None of this needs a call

Look at the list again. Every question can be answered in writing, before anyone books a video call. The call is for the two or three things only the two of you can settle, like whether you'd enjoy working together.

That makes it a good job for an AI. It's patient, it reads everything, it doesn't get awkward asking about money, and it doesn't skip a question because the other person seems nice.
